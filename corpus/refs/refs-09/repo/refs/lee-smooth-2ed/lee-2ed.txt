Chapter 21. Some Applications to Differential Geometry
21.1 Riemannian Metrics and Embeddings
In this chapter we collect several applications of the theory developed so far.
The reader is assumed to be familiar with the notion of a smooth manifold with
boundary, together with the basic properties of partitions of unity.
Throughout, all manifolds are assumed to be smooth, second countable, and
Hausdorff, and all maps are assumed to be smooth unless stated otherwise.
We begin with a preliminary observation about proper maps, which will be used
repeatedly in the arguments that follow.
Proposition 21.2. Let M be a smooth n-manifold with or without boundary.
The statement of the proposition is recorded on the following line.
Every smooth n-manifold admits a proper smooth embedding into some Euclidean space.
Proof. Choose a countable cover of M by regular coordinate balls, and let
{psi_i} be a smooth partition of unity subordinate to that cover. Each of the
coordinate charts may be composed with a suitable bump function so that the
resulting map is compactly supported.
Assembling these locally defined maps yields a smooth map into a Euclidean space
of possibly very large dimension, and a routine argument shows that the map so
obtained is injective on each element of the cover.
Properness follows from the fact that the exhaustion function constructed above
is proper by design, so preimages of compact sets are compact.
This completes the proof of the preliminary result.
Remark 21.3. The dimension produced by the argument above is not optimal, and
in general it grows with the number of charts required to cover the manifold.
The remainder of this section is devoted to reducing that dimension.
Exercise 21.4. Show that the hypothesis of second countability cannot be
dropped from the statement of the preceding proposition.
Exercise 21.5. Give an example of a manifold for which the naive construction
above produces an embedding into a space of dimension strictly greater than 2n.
21.2 The Whitney Theorems
The results of this section are due to Hassler Whitney, and are among the most
frequently quoted theorems in all of differential topology.
We first record a measure-theoretic lemma that is used in the proof.
Lemma 21.6 (Sard). If F is a smooth map between smooth manifolds, then the set
of critical values of F has measure zero in the codomain.
Proof. See the references at the end of the chapter; the proof is a careful
induction on the dimension of the domain and is omitted here.
Corollary 21.7. The set of regular values of a smooth map is dense.
Proof. Immediate from the preceding lemma, since a set of measure zero has
empty interior in a manifold.
Lemma 21.8. Let M be a smooth n-manifold and suppose F is a smooth map from M
into a Euclidean space of dimension strictly greater than 2n + 1.
Then almost every linear projection onto a hyperplane restricts to an injective
immersion on the image of F.
Proof. Consider the smooth map defined on the complement of the diagonal in the
product of M with itself, sending a pair of distinct points to the direction of
the line joining their images.
The domain of this map has dimension 2n, which is strictly less than the
dimension of the target projective space by hypothesis.
By the preceding corollary, the image of that map therefore omits at least one
direction, and any projection along an omitted direction is injective.
A similar argument applied to the unit sphere bundle of the tangent bundle shows
that almost every such projection is also an immersion.
Since the intersection of two sets of full measure again has full measure, a
single projection may be chosen with both properties simultaneously.
This proves the lemma.
Remark 21.9. The dimension bound in the preceding lemma is sharp for the
argument given, but not for the theorem that follows.
The reader should note carefully that repeated application of the lemma reduces
the ambient dimension one step at a time, and that the reduction stops as soon
as the dimension count in the proof no longer applies.
It is precisely this stopping point that accounts for the numerical bound
appearing in the statement of the main theorem below.
We are now ready to state and prove the principal result of this chapter.
Before doing so we fix some notation that will remain in force for the rest of
the section.
Let M denote a smooth n-manifold without boundary, and let N denote a smooth
manifold of dimension m, again without boundary.
All embeddings considered below are proper unless explicitly stated otherwise.
We write B for the open unit ball in Euclidean space of the relevant dimension,
and we write S for the corresponding unit sphere.
The letter k is reserved for indices ranging over a countable cover.
With these conventions in place, the statement of the theorem is as follows.
The proof occupies the remainder of this section and is broken into three steps.
Step one reduces the general case to the case of a compact manifold.
Step two applies the projection lemma repeatedly to reduce the dimension.
Step three verifies that the resulting map is proper.
Each step is carried out in detail below, and the reader who is willing to
accept the compact case may skip directly to the second step.
We remark that the argument given here differs slightly from Whitney's original
treatment, which proceeded by a direct construction.
The version presented here has the advantage of isolating the measure-theoretic
input into the single lemma proved above.
It has the disadvantage of producing a weaker bound in the non-compact case.
Both versions of the argument may be found in the literature cited at the end of
the chapter, and the interested reader is encouraged to compare them.
We now turn to the statement itself.
The following theorem is the central result of this chapter and is used without
further comment in the chapters that follow.
Its proof combines every result established so far in this section.
Readers primarily interested in applications may take the statement on faith and
proceed directly to the examples in the next section.
The examples there illustrate the sharpness of the dimension bound.
We state the theorem in the form in which it will be applied later.
Theorem 21.10 (Whitney Embedding Theorem). Every smooth n-manifold admits a
proper smooth embedding into Euclidean space of dimension 2n + 1.
Proof. By the first step of the reduction described above, it suffices to treat
the case in which M is compact.
In that case the preliminary proposition provides an embedding into a Euclidean
space of some finite dimension, possibly very large.
If that dimension exceeds 2n + 1, the projection lemma supplies a hyperplane
projection that remains an injective immersion.
Iterating the projection lemma reduces the dimension one step at a time until
the bound 2n + 1 is reached, at which point the lemma no longer applies.
An injective immersion of a compact manifold is an embedding, and an embedding
of a compact manifold is automatically proper.
This completes the proof in the compact case, and with it the proof of the
theorem.
